## Definition of K-Anonymity

K-Anonymity is a privacy model that ensures each record in a dataset is indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records based on quasi-identifiers (attributes like age, gender, or ZIP code that could potentially identify individuals).

## Sweeney and Origins

K-Anonymity was first introduced by Pierangela Samarati and Latanya Sweeney in 1998, with the goal to produce a release of person-specific data with scientific guarantees that the individuals who are the subjects of the data cannot be re-identified while the data remain practically useful.

## Quasi-Identifiers

Quasi-identifiers are a set of attributes from the dataset that can be used to identify an individual but are not unique to any individual; for example, in a patient medical history dataset, the patient's sex, age, and zip code are considered quasi-identifiers. These attributes can appear in other external datasets that are publicly available and can be easily leveraged by an adversary to re-identify an individual from the anonymized dataset.

## Re-Identification Risk

Around 87 percent of the US population can be uniquely identified with just their 5-digit zip code, gender, and date of birth taken together. Sweeney demonstrated exactly this by re-linking Massachusetts health insurance records to voter rolls in 1997.

## Implementation

The approach is based on generalizing attributes, and possibly deleting records, until each record becomes indistinguishable from at least k-1 others.

## The Three Criteria

The Opinion states that when re-identification remains possible through any means—whether technical, organizational, or practical—the data cannot be considered truly anonymous. # EDPB Guidelines 01/2025: Pseudonymisation and Identifiability

## Core Concept

The Guidelines adopt a **context-dependent approach** to identifiability. Data can constitute personal data to one party while functioning as anonymized information to another, depending on what linking information each party possesses.

## Key Principle on "Means Reasonably Likely to Be Used"

The EDPB emphasizes considering information "reasonably expected to be available" when assessing whether data remains identifiable. The article states that organizations must assess:

> "any additional information that may enable individual identification, including both information under control of the organisation and information outside of its control"

This reflects a subjective standard tied to specific actors' access and capabilities—not an absolute technical standard.

## Practical Application

**Same data, different status:** Pseudonymised data shared with a third party lacking the linking key represents personal data requiring protection obligations from that recipient. The Guidelines recognize that effective pseudonymisation limits "the group of individuals...to whom direct identifiers...are disclosed," establishing separate processing contexts with different identifiability conclusions depending on each party's actual access to additional information.
